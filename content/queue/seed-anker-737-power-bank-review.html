The output number is the one that matters

Wattage decides what a power bank can actually do. A 20W bank charges a phone. A 65W bank charges most ultrabooks at a usable speed. Anything around 100W or higher is what lets a 14-inch or 16-inch laptop charge while you are using it, rather than slowly losing ground.

This model's headline figure applies to a single port. That is normal across the category and rarely stated clearly in listings. Plug in a phone and a laptop together and the total is shared, so the laptop charges more slowly than it would alone. If you routinely charge two hungry devices at once, that split is the thing to think about, not the peak number.

  • Single-port peak is what the marketing number describes. Multi-port use divides it.
  • Your laptop's own limit applies too. A machine that only accepts 65W will not charge faster from a 140W source.
  • The cable matters. A thin or old USB-C cable will cap the whole chain regardless of what the bank can do.

What the screen changes

Four little LEDs telling you "somewhere between 25 and 50 percent" is the standard experience, and it is useless for planning. A display showing actual input and output wattage turns the bank into something you can reason about.

In practice this matters twice. The first is when you discover that a cable or a wall adapter is silently limiting you - the number on the screen makes that obvious in about ten seconds. The second is estimating recharge time, because a bank this size takes a while to refill from a weak charger.

Size and weight are the real cost

High capacity means a physical object you feel in a bag. This class of bank is closer to a small brick than a pocket accessory, and it will not disappear into a jeans pocket or a slim laptop sleeve.

Air travel is worth checking before you buy rather than at the gate. Lithium-ion packs above roughly 100 watt-hours have restrictions with most carriers, and large-capacity banks sit near that boundary. The packaging and the manufacturer's specification page state the watt-hour figure - confirm it against your airline's rules if you fly often.

Where the money is wasted

If everything you own charges at 20W or less - a phone, earbuds, a watch, an older tablet - then almost all of what this bank can do goes unused. A far cheaper 10,000mAh unit with modest output handles that load, weighs a third as much, and fits in a pocket.

The same is true if you are usually within reach of a socket. A power bank is a bridge between outlets, and if you rarely cross that gap, you are paying for capacity you will never drain.

What to check before you order

Three things, in order. Your laptop's maximum USB-C charging input (it is in the spec sheet, often lower than you expect). Whether the cable you already own is rated for high current - if it is not, budget for a proper one. And the airline rule if you travel.

  • Laptop input. If it is 65W or less, a mid-range bank is the better spend.
  • Cable rating. High-wattage charging needs a cable built for it.
  • Watt-hours. Find the figure and check it against your carrier's limit.
  • Recharge source. Filling 24,000mAh from a 20W wall charger is an overnight job.

Who should skip it

Phone-only users, people who commute with a laptop bag that goes near a socket every few hours, and anyone who wants something that lives in a pocket. There are honest, capable options at a fraction of the weight for those situations.

Questions buyers actually ask

Can a power bank like this charge a laptop while the laptop is in use?

Yes, if the bank's output on that port exceeds what the laptop draws under load. That is exactly what a high-wattage unit is for. A weaker bank will show the battery percentage holding steady or climbing very slowly instead of charging properly, which is the sign that the output is not enough.

Why does my laptop charge slower when I also plug in my phone?

Total output is shared between active ports. When a second device starts drawing current, the bank renegotiates and reduces what each port delivers. Charging the laptop alone will always be faster, so if speed matters, unplug the phone until the laptop is where you want it.

Is a 24,000mAh bank allowed on a plane?

It depends on the watt-hour rating, not the mAh figure, and the rules differ by carrier. Most airlines allow lithium-ion packs up to around 100 watt-hours in carry-on luggage without approval, and restrict anything larger. The watt-hour number is printed on the unit and in the specification sheet - check it before you fly.

How long does it take to recharge?

Entirely dependent on the wall charger you use. From a low-wattage phone adapter it is an overnight job; from a high-wattage USB-C charger it is a fraction of that. If you buy a large-capacity bank, buying a matching high-output wall charger is not optional - otherwise you trade one slow wait for another.

Do I need a special cable?

For high-wattage charging, usually yes. Cables are rated for a maximum current, and a thin or old cable will silently cap the whole chain. Look for a cable explicitly rated for 100W or higher, and if the display shows far less than expected, the cable is the first thing to swap.

The bottom line
