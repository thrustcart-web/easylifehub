# Choosing An Air Purifier: Six Things That Actually Matter

> Air purifier listings are full of numbers that mean nothing. These six are the ones that decide whether the machine does anything at all.

Air purifiers are unusually easy to buy badly, because the marketing vocabulary sounds technical while telling you almost nothing. "Advanced filtration", "medical grade", "99.97 percent" - all of it can be true and still describe a machine that will not clean the room you put it in.

There are six things worth checking. Everything else on the listing is decoration.

## One: CADR, matched to your room

Clean Air Delivery Rate is the only performance figure that means anything, because it measures how much clean air the machine actually delivers. It is published in cubic feet or cubic metres per minute, usually with separate numbers for dust, pollen and smoke.

The rule of thumb is straightforward: the unit should be able to turn over the air in your room several times an hour. Manufacturers publish a recommended room size, and it almost always assumes the lowest useful setting. If the bedroom is at the top of the stated range, expect it to run loudly to get there. Buy for a room somewhat larger than yours and the quiet setting becomes usable.

Be suspicious of any listing that gives a coverage number without a CADR figure. The coverage claim is doing work that the tested number should be doing.

## Two: the filter, and what it costs forever

A true HEPA filter is the standard to look for - it is a defined specification, not a marketing term. Anything described as "HEPA-type", "HEPA-style" or "HEPA-grade" is not the same thing and is usually a much coarser filter wearing a familiar word.

Then do the arithmetic nobody else does. Replacement filters are sold separately, they need changing every few months depending on run time and air quality, and they last for the whole life of the machine. Multiply the filter price by how often it changes and you have the true five-year cost. A cheap unit with expensive filters routinely costs more than the premium one it undercut.

- <strong>Look for "True HEPA"</strong> as a specification, not "HEPA-style" as a description.
- <strong>Check filter availability</strong> for that exact model - discontinued filters turn a working machine into a paperweight.
- <strong>Divide the filter price by its rated life</strong> to get a monthly running cost, then compare units on that basis.

## Three: noise at the setting you will use

Every purifier is quiet on its lowest setting and loud on its highest. The question that matters is where the useful airflow sits relative to the noise. A machine that only delivers meaningful CADR at its loudest speed will get switched off at night, which defeats the purpose in a bedroom.

Decibel figures alone are misleading because they do not describe the character of the sound. A low whoosh at a given level is far more tolerable than a whine or a rattle at the same level. Look for a dedicated sleep or night mode that dims the display as well as dropping the fan - a bright panel in a dark bedroom is its own problem.

## Four: what it cannot do

Worth stating plainly, because claims in this category overreach constantly. A particulate filter does not remove carbon monoxide. It does not meaningfully reduce humidity, and it will not fix a mould problem growing inside a wall - it can only reduce spores already airborne.

Activated carbon does adsorb some odours and volatile organic compounds, but the amount of carbon in a consumer unit is small and its useful life is short. Treat odour reduction as a modest bonus rather than a reason to buy. For a genuinely gassy problem - a new carpet, a paint smell, a garage - ventilation moves far more air than any purifier.

## Five: placement, which most people get wrong

An intake pressed against a wall or tucked behind a curtain starves the machine. So does putting it in the corner furthest from where you actually sit or sleep. Airflow needs clearance on the intake side and a path for the clean output to reach you.

In a bedroom, the practical answer is usually a metre or so from the bed with the intake side open, running on a medium setting overnight rather than blasting on high for an hour before you lie down. Continuous moderate operation beats intermittent full power, because particles keep arriving.

## Six: the smart features, honestly assessed

An auto mode with a particle sensor is genuinely useful - it responds to cooking or a windy day without you thinking about it, and it keeps energy use down. That is the one smart feature worth paying for.

App control is a convenience, not a capability, and it carries a dependency: if the manufacturer stops maintaining the app or shuts the cloud service, that feature disappears while the fan keeps working. If a unit requires an app for basic scheduling, treat that as a design flaw.

## Pros

- A true HEPA unit measurably reduces airborne particulate in a sealed room
- Genuinely useful for allergy sufferers and households with pets
- Auto mode with a sensor removes the need to think about it
- Quiet sleep modes make continuous overnight running practical

## Cons

- Filter replacements are a permanent running cost
- Does nothing for carbon monoxide, humidity or mould inside walls
- Carbon filters for odours are small and short-lived
- Coverage claims on listings routinely assume the lowest setting

## FAQ

**How big a room can an air purifier actually handle?**

Use the CADR figure rather than the coverage claim. As a working method, calculate the room's volume in cubic feet and aim for the unit to move that volume several times per hour at the setting you will tolerate. The published room-size numbers almost always assume the lowest fan speed, which is why a machine rated for a large room can feel ineffective in it.

**Are HEPA and True HEPA different?**

Yes. True HEPA refers to a defined filtration standard for particulate capture. HEPA-type, HEPA-style and HEPA-grade are marketing descriptions with no specification behind them, and they generally describe a coarser filter. If the listing avoids the words True HEPA, assume it is not one.

**How often do filters need changing?**

Typically every few months of continuous use, less often if the machine runs a few hours a day in clean air. Homes with pets, smokers, construction dust nearby or high pollen counts go through them faster. Most units have a filter-life indicator based on run time rather than actual loading, so treat it as a reminder, not a measurement.

**Will an air purifier get rid of cooking or pet smells?**

Partly, and less than the marketing implies. Odours are gases, handled by activated carbon rather than the particulate filter, and consumer carbon loads are small with a short useful life. You will notice a reduction; you will not eliminate a persistent source. Ventilation and removing the source do more work than any purifier.

**Should I run it all the time?**

For an allergy or pet-hair problem, yes - continuous operation on a low or medium setting outperforms short bursts on high, because particles are generated continuously. Modern units draw little power on low settings, so the running cost is usually far smaller than people expect. Check the wattage on the lowest setting to confirm.

**Do ionisers and UV lights add anything?**

Treat both as optional at best. Some ionisers generate ozone as a by-product, which is a respiratory irritant and the last thing you want a bedroom appliance producing. UV stages need sufficient dwell time to inactivate anything, which consumer airflow rates rarely allow. A good mechanical filter is the part doing the work.

## Conclusion

Ignore the coverage claim, find the CADR, confirm the filter is true HEPA, and price the replacements over five years. Those four checks eliminate most of the bad purchases in this category before you ever compare brands.
Then buy slightly more capacity than your room needs. The margin is what lets you run the machine quietly enough to leave it on, and leaving it on is the entire point.
